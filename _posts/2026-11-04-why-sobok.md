SOBOK doesn't have a grand meaning. But the reason I made it does.

Koreans living in Toronto miss the desserts from home. Korean desserts have their own thing: not too sweet, nutty, savoury almost, and you can bend them into Korean flavours — mugwort, black sesame, injeolmi, smoked cheese. How is that not attractive?

I've always eaten more dessert and snacks than actual meals. Still do. Somewhere in there I started wondering why a good dessert tastes good. My conclusion: it's the sincerity of the person who made it, their conviction about it, their love for it, and then the face of the person eating it. You see them happy, you sync with it, and you're happy too. That's what makes a good dessert taste better than it technically is.

## Why financiers specifically

Because I love what the word means. Financiers are shaped like gold bars. People give them at New Year to say "make lots of money this year." And the ingredients are absurdly good: egg whites and almond flour, buttery, nutty, crisp.

I love my financiers. I did a stupid amount of research to get the outside shattering-crisp and the inside dense but not soggy, still soft. I want people to taste what came out of all that and be happy.

## How it started

It started as GUUM in spring 2026, and the rule was "same-day bake, same-day sharing." I only charged cost. The first paid batch went badly: the batter kept boiling over in the oven the night before delivery. "This has never happened to me." I rebuilt every recipe for structure that night, three of each flavour, one flavour at a time. Lesson: never mix all the batters at once.

My first thank-you post after that round said I was surprised and giddy that so many people cared, that I'd obsessed over every detail because I wanted it to look good, and thank you for liking the packaging. That's still how I feel.

## Why I kept going

Because I wanted to learn how to run something properly, so it would stay mine. "What do I need to know when I make something and sell it?" "How do I build a profit structure — what price covers the money and time I put in?" Those were my actual questions in June. The answers turned into a cost model that showed my $20 set made $14 in cash and $3 after labour, a workbook that tracks eighteen ingredients, and a list of ten regulations I had to satisfy before selling again.

That list is where the bakery turned into something else. I'd rather do it properly than get thrown out for doing it illegally. So I'm doing it properly, which is slower, and which is the whole point.

I'm not trying to get rich on financiers. I bake once a month. If a post went viral and two hundred orders came in, I couldn't make them. I just want the twenty boxes I do make to be the best thing someone eats that week.
